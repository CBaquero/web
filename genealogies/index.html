Intellectual Genealogies

What became of a paper’s ideas.

Concept by Carlos Baquero (FEUP, Universidade do Porto & INESC TEC) · Execution by Claude agents

The impact of a paper should not be measured by citation counts: wrong papers are cited too, and the most influential ones end up referred to by the name of their concept, often with no citation at all. What counts is what happened to the ideas inside. Each report in this series takes one seminal paper of computer science and follows its ideas forward through the record: which concepts traveled and under what names, which were reinvented by people who had not read the original, which stalled, and which now run in production systems that no longer name their source. The unit of analysis is the idea, not the citation.

Renamed into Invisibility

What became of Simula

Seed paper: Ole-Johan Dahl, Kristen Nygaard. “SIMULA — an ALGOL-Based Simulation Language.” Communications of the ACM 9(9), September 1966 — with the 1967 “Class and Subclass Declarations” as seed cluster.

An eight-page “simulation language” paper of 1966 carries the semantics of what would later be called object-oriented programming; a companion paper of 1967 renamed everything in it. Sixty years later the concepts run in essentially every mainstream language, uncredited — and the seed’s citation curve is the first in this series that declines.

No. 1 · draft · August 2026

The Clock That Ran Away

What became of “Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events”

Seed paper: Leslie Lamport. “Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System.” Communications of the ACM 21(7), July 1978.

An eight-page paper with four references — two of them relativity textbooks — defined what “before” means in a system with no common time. Forty-eight years later its concepts run in language memory models, databases, and blockchains, mostly without citation, and the passage its first readers skipped over became its highest-impact idea.

No. 0 · pilot · draft · August 2026

The Long Convergence

What became of the CRDT idea

Seed paper: Marc Shapiro, Nuno Preguiça, Carlos Baquero, Marek Zawirski. “Conflict-Free Replicated Data Types.” SSS 2011, LNCS 6976.

Fifteen years after four researchers proposed that replicas can converge without synchronization, the founding paper’s citation curve has still not bent downward, and the acronym names technology shipped in databases and collaborative editors. The usual origin story omits a correctness crisis in a neighbouring community; this report puts it back.

Pilot report. The seed paper is co-authored by the series director; the analysis was completed blind, before any correction from him was taken, and his corrections are logged as validation data. The pilot exists to test the method on a lineage the validator knows from the inside.

How these reports are made

The research and the writing are done by an AI system — Claude, by Anthropic — running a written, versioned methodology under the direction of Carlos Baquero, who commissions each report, reviews it, and validates it against the record. Citation data comes from OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar; full texts are read before any lineage claim is made. Every claim in a report is tied to a ledger entry whose confidence class — documented, strong, plausible, or resemblance — is the strongest the evidence supports, and the prose is required to match it. A methods paper describing the pipeline is in preparation.

Each report page has a comment section backed by GitHub Discussions; a GitHub account is needed to post. Corrections are welcome: a genealogy is a set of claims about the record, and the record can push back.
